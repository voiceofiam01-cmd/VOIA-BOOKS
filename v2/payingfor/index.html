What Are You
Paying For?

A phone vibrates. A bank alert. Credit or debit. For a moment an amount on a screen can sound louder than a sermon. Credit can become evidence that God has remembered you. Debit can become proof that you have entered a covenant. The absence of an alert can turn inward: your faith is weak; your enemies are stronger; your ancestors are displeased; you have not given enough.

The figure is real. The interpretation is not printed inside it. A credit can be wages, a gift, a loan, a refund, a business payment, an error, or a fraud. A debit can be rent, food, medicine, school fees, generosity, exploitation, or worship. The transaction records movement. It does not explain the universe.

This book is not an argument against Christianity, Islam, Odinala, ancestral practice, prayer, offerings, sacrifice, zakat, tithing, or religious community. It is an argument against the use of sacred authority to create financial fear.

A church may need money. A mosque may need money. A shrine, temple, prayer house, school, charity, or spiritual practitioner may need money. Leaders may be paid for labour. Buildings require maintenance. Workers deserve wages. None of that settles the moral question. The question is not whether money enters spiritual life — it always has. The question is what money is doing when it enters. Is it supporting work? Relieving suffering? Expressing gratitude? Or is it purchasing access? Being demanded under threat? Being sold as an investment with a guaranteed divine return?

Money can support spiritual work. It cannot purchase truth.

That is the standard of the book. It is not a standard that asks anyone to stop giving. It is a standard that asks the giver to remain free.

A tithe may be chosen. An offering may be voluntary. A sacrifice may be meaningful. Zakat may be a serious obligation. A practitioner may be paid. A community may collect. But none of these practices makes coercion sacred.

A spiritual life that requires fear to finance it has already confused power with truth.— Author's Note; Part I closing
Transactional spirituality

When money becomes a remote control for ultimate reality.

There is a specific structure that repeats across traditions and across continents. A spiritual authority offers an explanation for personal trouble, connects the explanation to a product or payment or rite, and places money between the believer and the desired result. The payment does not merely support an institution. It becomes part of the alleged mechanism of rescue.

When the promise succeeds, the authority receives credit. When it fails, the payer is blamed — the faith was insufficient, the instructions were not followed, an enemy interfered, a secret sin remained, the payment was too small, another payment is required. The promise protects itself by transferring failure to the person who paid.

This book gives that structure a name — transactional spirituality: the reduction of sacred relationship to exchange, where money, obedience, or ritual performance is offered in expectation of a controlled spiritual return. Its opposite is not a spirituality without money or institutions. It is a spirituality that refuses to treat money as a remote control for ultimate reality.

Neither flesh nor blood reaches Allah; piety is what reaches Him. (Qur'an 22:37)

I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings. (Hosea 6:6)

Two traditions, separated by centuries and languages, arrive at the same narrow point: the material act has meaning, but the material substance is not a bribe.— Chapter 3, Sacrifice and the Price of Access

Questions

The ones before you buy.

Will this book make me cynical about all spiritual giving?

No. It is written against cynicism as much as against coercion. The closing section is about anti-transactional spirituality — what generosity looks like when fear has been removed. You may give more, not less, after reading it. You will give differently.

Are you calling my pastor a fraud?

No. The book is explicit that a religious institution can collect money transparently, pay its workers, support the poor, and do genuine good. The framework is designed to help you distinguish that institution from one that is financing itself through manufactured fear. The distinction is not "all leaders are good / all leaders are bad." It is a set of questions.

Do you name names?

Publicly reported cases are cited by name with [D] marks and source references. Unconvicted people are not called criminals. Most of the book uses structural analysis rather than individual shaming — because the structure is what repeats, even when individuals change.

Is this for Christians only?

No. Part I treats tithe and offering (Christian); Part I also treats zakat and sadaqah (Islam); Parts III and VI treat traditional practice and dibia work (Odinala). The transactional structure is shown to operate in all three; the safeguards in all three are identified as well.

Does the book say I should never give a spiritual leader money?

Absolutely not. Workers deserve wages. A practitioner deserves payment for time and labour. A community deserves support. The book asks: what exactly is being paid for, is the purpose specific, can you decline safely, is there accountability, and does the payment rely on fear?

How long is it really?

~30,000 words — about 150 pages. Read the framework first (Parts I–II), then use the rest as reference when you need it.
