The 3 A.M.
Question.

It starts the same way. The phone goes dark. The house is quiet. You should be asleep. And then the ceiling becomes the most interesting thing in the world.

One night, in Abuja, the author asked his wife a question. If every authority were removed — every restriction lifted, no fear, no panic, nothing holding you back — what question would you ask? He thought she'd say something about children, or travel, or the house. She thought for a long time in the dark and she said: "Why must we die?"

That question became a 106,000-word book — biology, philosophy, African cosmology, neuroscience, grief. This is not that book.

This book is for 3 a.m. It is seven nights, ten minutes each, a pen and a piece of paper. It is not philosophy. It is not theology. It is not therapy. It is what an older brother might tell you if he sat on your couch at 3 a.m. and said: I know. I know it is loud right now. Let me show you something that helps.

You do not need to answer the question at 3 a.m. You need to set it down gently so you can close your eyes and rest and wake up and live your life.

You are lying in bed. Your body is tired. Your mind should be resting, carrying the good message forward — the message that says you are alive, you are here, you have things to do and people to love and a morning waiting. That message is fast. That message is true. That message is the dog.

But something else arrives first. The anxious thought. The tight chest. The spiral. What if I get sick? What is the point of any of this if it all ends? Why must we die? That thought is slower, quieter, more deliberate. It does not shout. It whispers. It keeps going while the dog is eating. And it arrives first.

Your anxiety at 3 a.m. is the toad. It is not the truth. It is the wrong message arriving before the right one.— Day 1, Name the Message
What the practices actually do

The thought is a passenger,
not the driver.

Each night's practice is built on the same discovery: the 3 a.m. question does not live in your mind. It lives in your body. You cannot out-think a body. At 3 a.m. your mind is an unreliable narrator — everything it produces is distorted, amplified, catastrophic. But the body is honest, and the body can be reached.

Over seven nights you learn to name the message, locate where it lives in the body, remember izu oke (wholeness, not perfection), pull out a photograph of the people you love, name the names you carry, write three sentences, and then — on the seventh night — learn when to stop.

When a child is frightened at night, the grandmother does not argue with the child's fears. She does not say: Let me explain the cultural function of masquerades. She puts her hand on the child's chest. She breathes slowly, and the child's breathing follows hers — because the body learns from the body beside it.— Day 2, Where You Feel It

Questions

The ones before you buy.

Will this cure my death anxiety?

No. Death anxiety does not have a cure. What this book gives you is a reliable way to set the question down at 3 a.m. so it does not ruin your sleep. In daylight the question returns on different terms, and the long book is there for that.

Is this religious? Do I have to be Igbo or Christian?

No. The practices draw on Igbo stories (Dog and Toad, izu oke) because those are the stories the author has inside him, but the exercises themselves (naming, body-scan, breathwork, photographs, three sentences) are available to anybody.

Do I need Why Must We Die? to use this?

No. This is a standalone companion. Many readers will buy this first because they are tired tonight, and then later, when they are ready for the deeper investigation, they will buy the long book. The order does not matter.

Can I use this for other 3 a.m. anxieties — not just death?

Yes. The framework (name the message, locate it in the body, ground it in what is true, set it down) works for money anxiety, health anxiety, relationship anxiety, and any other 3 a.m. spiral. Death is the form the author knows best, which is why the practices are framed around it.

Is this suitable for someone who is grieving right now?

Use your judgment. The practices are gentle and do not argue with grief. But if you have lost someone in the last weeks, read the first two nights and see. If it does not feel right, set it aside. The refund is unconditional.

How long is it really?

~10,000 words — about 50 pages. One short chapter per night. You can read the whole book in forty minutes, but the point is not to finish it; it is to do one practice per night for seven nights.

Are there worksheets?

Yes — a printable body map and a printable three-sentences card, designed to be kept beside your bed.
