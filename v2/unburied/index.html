The Unburied
Questions.

In 1992, riding a motorcycle taxi on an Enugu road, his mother was in an accident. A windscreen shattered. A piece of glass went into her cheek. It was not, on the day, the most urgent injury in the chaos. The triage priorities were elsewhere. The wound closed over. The glass stayed.

It stayed for eleven years. Not painless — he does not believe it was painless — but familiar. A woman raising children largely alone, farming, teaching, navigating a marriage that had already produced one public accusation of witchcraft against her, does not necessarily have the luxury of treating a small deep facial injury as an emergency. She had bigger fires. The shard became one more thing she carried, the way you learn to walk around a piece of furniture you've stopped noticing.

In 2003 the glass was surgically extracted. The scar faded until even someone looking for it could not quite find it. There was another wound, though, that no surgeon ever removed. The accusation. The oracle's silence cleared her name in the eyes of custom, the way a wound might close over on the surface. But something remained lodged beneath that closure for years — a residual suspicion, present enough to be felt, not visible enough to justify complaint, never formally extracted.

This book is about that category of thing — the questions a family buries, the injuries it walls off, the patterns it passes down without ever naming them. It is about a boy born into the aftermath. It is about his brother Chukwunonso, who did not survive. It is about sickle-cell and the malaria trade-off, about ogbanje and the sociology of scapegoating, about Jung and the river beneath the self, about generators and zinc roofs, about what the body remembers that the mouth has forgotten. It is not psychology. It is not tradition. It is both, held up to the light, with neither allowed to claim it has the final answer.

Bodies and reputations carry old injuries in remarkably similar ways: both prefer to heal over rather than fully resolve, both can remain functional for years while quietly harboring something sharp beneath the surface, and both, if left unaddressed long enough, eventually require someone willing to go looking with the right instruments to finally take the old thing out. — Chapter 4, The 1992 Shard
What gets inherited

The body walls off what it cannot resolve.
So does a family.

A retained foreign body — glass, metal, wood, debris left in tissue after injury — gets encapsulated by fibrous scar tissue. The surrounding wound closes and functions normally while the fragment remains beneath the surface, sometimes for decades, sometimes producing no symptoms at all, sometimes producing chronic low-grade discomfort easy for both patient and physician to underestimate.

Something very like that happens to families. The thing that cannot be spoken gets walled off. Life continues on the surface. Children grow up learning to walk around the sealed-off room the way they learn to walk around a piece of furniture. They can feel that something is there — they learn the facial expression that means don't ask, the subject that gets changed at a certain mention, the aunt whose name is spoken differently — but nobody tells them what it is, and eventually they stop asking.

There is a specific, particular grief that belongs to children who lose a parent before they are mature enough to have asked the questions that parent's life deserved. I did not get to ask my mother, as an adult, what those eleven years actually felt like from the inside. What I have instead of her answer is this chapter. — Chapter 4, What I Did Not Know to Ask Her
You will recognise this if

Any of these sound like you or someone you carry.

The questions you were never allowed to ask have started asking themselves.

  • You are carrying a wound no one in your family will name — a death nobody talks about, a feud nobody explains, an aunt whose name changes the room.
  • You have reacted to your partner, your child, or your boss with an intensity that surprises you, and later you realise you were not reacting to them at all.
  • There is a facial expression, a tone of voice, a way of sighing, that you learned from a parent and have caught yourself using in exactly the same situations.
  • You have patterns in your relationships — the same argument, the same distance, the same exit — that you can see clearly in hindsight and cannot stop in the moment.
  • You find yourself attracted to churches or prophets or men who promise you certainty, and you suspect the attraction has less to do with truth than with how badly you want a clean answer.
  • You know the bare facts of a family story and you have begun to suspect there is a whole other version.
  • You have read books about "inner healing" or "generational curses" and walked away unsatisfied — the formulas felt borrowed, the explanations too clean for the thing you actually carry.

The cost of leaving it buried.

Encapsulated shards do not kill you. That is what makes them dangerous — because you can live with them for a decade, or a lifetime, without realising the accommodation they have cost you:

You pass on to your children a tone of voice you thought you were choosing freely. You choose partners who reproduce the exact dynamics you promised yourself you would never repeat. You cannot enter certain rooms, or certain subjects, without a chronic low-grade tension you have mistaken for personality. You reach for religious certainty, or political certainty, or therapeutic certainty, because a sealed question is more comfortable than an open one. You lose the brother or sister or parent a second time — once to the event, once to the story the family agreed to tell about it afterwards.

The trade-off is written into the blood. The same gene that gives a child malaria protection in the dangerous first years of life is the gene that can kill that child's sibling if that child inherits two copies. There is no villain here. There is a deal — a very old deal, struck between a species and a parasite long before any of us were born, a deal in which some children are born with protection and some children are born with a sentence, and most mothers in malaria country, standing over a grave, will not be told there was mathematics at work in their loss. — Chapter 7, The Biology of the Genetic Trade-off
Pluralism, not pick-a-side

He refuses one vocabulary.
That refusal is the book's central argument.

There are readers who want this to be a traditional book — about ancestors, ogbanje, the oracle, the river beneath, unbroken lineage. There are readers who want this to be a psychology book — intergenerational trauma, somatic markers, attachment, encapsulation, the talking cure. There are readers who want this to be a Christian book in which prayer resolves every buried thing.

It is not any one of those books. It will not pick a side. The author spent years moving between those vocabularies. He sat under the ukwu ube tree with cowries and he sat in hospital waiting rooms and he sat in churches and he sat with the mathematics and he found that none of them alone explains what a mother's eleven-year shard is, or why the body remembers, or what a brother who died at three days old leaves in the bones of the siblings who survive.

This is not a book about answers. It is a book about questions that were buried — in a grandmother's silence, in a mother's cheek, in a brother's absence, in the body of a child who inherited them all. The author does not resolve these questions. He digs them up. If you need a book that picks one vocabulary and defends it against the others — if you need Jesus to be the answer, or tradition to be the answer, or psychology to be the answer — there are many. If you want a book that lets them all sit on the same table, and shows you what happens when they do, this is that book.

About the author

He is not writing from the far side of healing.

M

Munachimso E. Nwagbara was born in Enugu and lives between the city and Mgbowo, where he keeps the house his father left him under an ukwu ube tree. He has a mathematics degree, a supply company, a wife, two children, and — since his twenties — the strange, unasked-for practice of sitting with cowries and telling people true things about themselves he has no ordinary way of knowing.

This is the book he did not want to write. He wrote it because the questions would not stay buried. His mother is dead. His brother Collins is still missing. He has not "processed" everything in these pages, and will not pretend otherwise. He writes as a son, a brother, a mathematician, a man who has sat under the tree long enough to know what he does not know.

Book One (The Watching Mind) teaches you to watch. Book Two is what you find when you start digging. Formally the books stand alone. Practically, read Watching first.

Questions

The ones before you buy.

Is this a traditionalist / African spirituality book?

It is a book by an Igbo man about what Igbo families carry. It writes frankly about oracles, ogbanje, chi, and sacred trees because those things are part of the material, not because it is advocating them as a system. He is equally frank about the church he grew up in, the neuroscience he has read, the Jung he argues with, and the mathematics.

Is this a Christian book?

The author is a Christian. The book does not resolve to Christian answers, or to anti-Christian answers. Chapter 17 — "When the Churches Closed" — is one of the more direct things written in the Longform about what the church was and was not able to do in the face of the things he is describing. Readers from many traditions have written to say the book respected their own.

Is this a trauma book / a therapy book?

He draws on somatic trauma work (van der Kolk appears in Chapter 5) and on clinical medicine (retained foreign bodies, encapsulated scar tissue, sickle-cell genetics). He does not pretend to be a therapist. If the thing you carry is active and wrecking your sleep, your marriage, or your capacity to work, this book is not a substitute for a clinician. It is a companion — something to read alongside, with both feet on the ground.

Do I need to read The Watching Mind first?

Formally no. Practically, yes. If you cannot yet step back and notice a thought as an object rather than a truth, the material in this book will become either a weapon you use against your family or a pity party you throw for yourself. Book One teaches you the basic move. Book Two is what you do with it.

How long is it really?

~94,000 words — about 480 pages. It is the longest Longform to date. Read one or two chapters at a sitting. Pause after Book I. Do not rush the glass-shard chapter.

What if my family is fine and there's nothing buried?

Then read it anyway. The book is not only for people with obvious wounds. Every family has something it walled off. The question is whether you have found it yet. Many readers write to say they thought they came from "an ordinary family with no drama" and finished the book knowing differently.

Does this book say African families are broken?

It says African families are human families, and human families carry things. There is no special indictment here. The specific forms are specific to his people; the underlying mechanisms — scapegoating, encapsulation, inherited pattern, grief without permission to name it — are universal.

Is this book going to make me angry at my parents?

It may. It also gives you, in the second half, a way not to stay angry. Understanding what was walled off is not the same as prosecuting the people who walled it. They were often carrying the same shard.
