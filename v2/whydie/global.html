Why Must
We Die?

In the time before death, when human beings could still speak face to face with Chukwu, something terrible happened for the first time. A person fell down and did not get up. The body grew cold. The breath stopped. They had never seen this before. There was no word for what had happened, because nothing like it had ever happened.

Confused and frightened, the people held a council beneath the ogirisi tree for seven days. On the eighth day they chose two messengers. To the dog — fast, strong, loyal — they gave the hopeful message: tell Chukwu that when a person falls this way, the person should wake up; death should be like sleep, temporary, brief, followed by rising. To the toad — slow, deliberate, persistent — they gave the darker message: tell Chukwu that when a person falls, the person should not wake up; death should be permanent, final, without return.

They gave the positive message to the faster messenger. The dog would arrive first. But on the way the dog passed a compound where food was being prepared — roasted yam, palm oil, the rich scent of ogiri fermenting in clay pots. He stopped. He ate. The errand slipped from his mind the way water slips through open fingers. The toad did not stop. Hop by hop, hour by hour, he delivered his message. Chukwu granted the first message to arrive. Divine decrees, once spoken, cannot be withdrawn.

And so death entered the world. Not through divine design. Not through human sin. Through a dog's appetite and a toad's persistence and a message that arrived out of order.

The sentence "Why must we die?" contains four words. Each one is a trap. Why conflates three questions — causal, functional, existential — and most people ask the third and receive the first. Must implies necessity when what we actually have is universality. We seems obvious until you ask whether it includes bacteria, jellyfish, bristlecone pines, or only humans. Die seems like a single event until you learn the heart, the brain, the cells, and consciousness all die at different times and in different ways. Death is not a moment. It is a cascade. This book takes each word apart.

The Dog and the Toad is not an epigraph. It is not decoration. It is a claim made by a tradition that has been thinking about death for millennia: that death is not necessary, not woven into the fabric of existence, not the plan. Death happened — contingently, through error, through a failure of logistics. The world we live in is the result of a message that arrived out of order. That claim will be tested, challenged, revisited, and transformed across the course of this book. — Prologue, The Day Death Became a Question
I used to believe that after death there was heaven or hell. Somewhere along the way, that belief dissolved — not through argument, not through trauma, but gradually, the way morning mist thins under a rising sun, until one morning you reach for it and it is simply not there. I did not replace it with the opposite conviction either. I do not believe that death leads to nothing. I have not concluded that consciousness ends when the brain stops. I have concluded nothing. I simply do not know. — Prologue
From the cell to the funeral

Science does its work. Philosophy does its work. Grief does its work. The question remains.

The book is organised in five parts that move outward in widening circles, from the smallest unit to the largest question:

Part I takes the sentence apart. Four chapters, one per word. Why? Must? We? Die? Each word is shown to carry a trap — a conflation, an assumption, a category error, a mis-timing. Most arguments about death never get past these four traps.

Part II is the body. Eleven chapters from biology, evolution, oncology, and gerontology. Everything dies (but not quite everything). A body built to break. The cell that refused to die (cancer). The price of repair (senescence). The immortal worm. The shape of the selective shadow. Our denial. Transition and grief. A first summing.

Part III is the human. The animal that knows. The first funeral. The invention of the afterlife. The fear beneath the fear. Building things that outlive us. The doctor who could not save himself. How do we want to die? Then an interlude set in a compound — the place where the living and the dead share space.

Part IV is the mind. Where are you? The brain makes a person. The hard problem of consciousness. When the brain stops. Near-death experiences. Can you survive without your body? The man who comes back. The Igbo question — what exactly returns? The person who does not end at the skin. The ancestors are not a scientific hypothesis. Chukwu without the human witness.

Part V is the unknown. The immortality industry. If we could live forever. The universe does not promise us forever. Evil without death. Why is there something that can die? The strange fact that we are here. We wanted an answer. What we actually know. What if the question was wrong? Then an epilogue.

  • You are tired of books that take you straight to a conclusion they had reached before page one.

What the false certainties cost us.

When we answer the question too quickly, we do not only mis-state the truth. We damage people:

The bereaved are told their loved one is "in a better place" before they have been allowed to feel that the place they are in is worse. The dying are denied honest conversation because the people around them cannot tolerate their own uncertainty. The young are handed either a fear-based eschatology or a glib materialism, and both collapse the first time a real death arrives. Medical decisions at the end of life are made by people who have not done the work of deciding what they actually believe death is. Grief is pathologised by one tradition and theologised away by another, and neither lets the grieving person simply sit with what they have lost.

About the author

He is writing as someone who does not know.

M

Munachimso E. Nwagbara wrote this book because he had to. He lost people he loved. He sat beside graves. He watched the doctrine of his childhood dissolve the way mist dissolves — not in argument, but under the slow unrelenting light of ordinary mornings. He did not replace it with the opposite doctrine. He arrived, instead, at a not-knowing that turned out not to be a torment.

He has spent years reading the biology, the philosophy, the traditions, the neuroscience, the near-death literature, and the Igbo accounts. He writes from Mgbowo, Enugu, from inside the work rather than from a settled position.

Book Nine (Who Is God?) is recommended but not required. The two books are companions; this one takes a single sentence and works it the way Book Nine works "God." Book Seven (Alibi) is also useful, because a lot of what people believe about death is alibi, not investigation.

Questions

The ones before you buy.

Does this book conclude that there is an afterlife, or that there isn't?

Neither. The author says plainly: "I have concluded nothing. I simply do not know." The chapters on consciousness, near-death, ancestors, and ilọ ụwa take the claims seriously without pretending they prove survival. The chapters on neuroscience take the evidence seriously without pretending it proves extinction. Both sides' overstatements are flagged.

Is this a Christian book? An Igbo traditional book?

It is a book written by an Igbo man who was raised Christian and who has read widely in both traditions and in the secular literature. It does not argue for either as a final answer. It treats Christian eschatology, Igbo ideas of ilọ uwa and ndichie, philosophical materialism, transhumanist longevity, and secular existentialism as five serious positions, and subjects each one to the same scrutiny.

Will reading this make me more afraid of death?

It may, for a season. Chapter 19 names the fear beneath the fear, and the honest answer is that not-knowing does not always feel like liberation. Many readers have reported the opposite — that after working through the book the fear became a companion rather than an enemy. The author does not promise comfort. He promises clarity.

I am grieving right now. Is this a good time?

Possibly not. The book does not rush past grief, but it does not hold your hand either. Wait until you can read carefully. The book will still be here. If you buy it now and discover the timing is wrong, come back to it in three months or a year; the lifetime-update guarantee applies regardless.

How technical is the science?

Written for an educated lay reader. There are chapters on cancer biology, telomeres, senescence, and the neural correlates of consciousness, but the author is a careful explainer — no prior biology or philosophy training required.

How long is it really?

~106,000 words — about 530 pages. This is not a book to read straight through in a weekend. One chapter a sitting. Pause at the interlude. Let the Compound chapter breathe. You will know when you are ready for Part V.

Do I need to read Book Nine first?

No. The two books share a method and a posture, but they address different questions and stand independently. If you find yourself wanting more on the metaphysical framing after finishing this book, Book Nine will be there.

If you don't give an answer, what am I buying?

A way to ask the question more carefully. The author's claim is not that he has solved death; it is that most of what passes for thinking about death is a series of evasions, and that clearing those evasions away is itself worth the price of the book. The epilogue names what you are left with after the evasions are cleared.
