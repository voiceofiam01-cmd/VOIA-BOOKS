What no one says out loud

Awakening is not the thing that happens to you.
It is the thing you do with what arrived.

It usually starts at night. Not always. But often at night. The house is quiet, the phone is face down, and there is nobody left to perform for. And the question arrives. It is never loud. It is small, and it is exact:

What am I doing?
And then a second one, worse.Who asked me to?

That question is the awakening. Not the light. Not the tears. Not the vision at a workshop you paid four hundred dollars for. Those things happen to people. Some of them are real. But they are symptoms. The thing itself is quieter and much more dangerous.

The thing itself is a question you cannot put down.

Before it, you lived on borrowed answers. Your mother's answers. Your church's answers. Your profession's answers. You never examined them because you never needed to. Then one night the borrowing stops working. The answers did not change. You started asking.

The hard sentences

What this book says plainly that every other book on the subject hides.

  • Wanting to die is not a stage of spiritual awakening. It is an emergency. It deserves the same response as chest pain. Appendix A is one page and it is the most important page in the book.
  • Spiritual awakening is not for the weak is a lie. It is for the weak. Weakness is available. It is the only honest starting point any of us has.
  • The same force produces the healer and the madman. The difference is consent. Refusing a call does not switch it off — it reroutes it. Into the body, the drinking, the third business, the rage that has no proportion.
  • You are not too broken to begin. You are broken in the exact place the question gets in.
  • Awakening does not pay rent. Keep the job while you see through the job. Feed the children while you question the marriage. The work is done in the margins of an ordinary life.
  • A call is a debt, not an honour. In the author's tradition, nobody throws a party because Agwu called them. They tighten their belt.
A note on responsibility. In 2008, in two Nigerian states, an estimated fifteen thousand children were accused of witchcraft — driven out, starved, harmed, killed — because a culture lost the people who could tell a call from a crisis. Questions

The ones you are asking.

Is this a Christian book? An Igbo book? A self-help book?

It is a book about spiritual awakening written from an Igbo Christian life that has also read Western philosophy, been broken by its own tradition, and refused to fake synthesis. It quotes scripture when scripture works as a manual. It quotes Igbo tradition when tradition describes what the author has seen. It does not subordinate either to the other.

Do I have to be Igbo or African to read it?

No. The book uses Igbo material because that is where the author stands, and because the category "spiritual awakening" has been largely emptied of rigour by a Western industry selling the experience. The definitions, the warnings, and the sequence hold outside Africa.

Is this therapy? Is it medical advice?

No. It says so in the opening and says so again in the crisis appendix. If you are unwell, be treated. If you are in crisis, read Appendix A first — one page — and then get help. The book will wait.

Will it convert me to anything?

No. It will ask you to answer the question that arrived. That answer is yours and it will look like your life, not like the author's.

How long is it?

Roughly 20,000 words — about 110 typeset pages. Short enough to read in two sittings. Dense enough that you will return to parts.
