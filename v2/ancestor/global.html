That is half the picture. It is the half that looks over your shoulder.

The sentence the book builds to
A god is not sent. A god is started. — Chapter Three, Root

When you were born, they buried your cord in the ground and planted a tree over it. Nkwu alo. The tree of life. It secures land. It confirms blood. It binds you to Ala, the earth. You did not plant it. You did not choose the spot. Nobody consulted you.

Somebody planted you.

When a man dies, they destroy his chi shrine — the personal shrine to his own life force. They do not destroy his Agwu shrine. That one stays. His children keep it. Their children keep it. For generations.

There is a part of you that will not survive your own funeral, and a part that will. You are building both right now, with what you do with your life. The question is not will I be remembered. The question is which shrine are they keeping.

How the book moves

Seven chapters. One tree.

The book is built the way a tree is built. Read it in order. It is short — about eighty minutes — and written that way on purpose.

SeedYou already have a tree. Nkwu alo. The difference between chi (destroyed at death) and Agwu (kept by your children). The two rites — Ime Agwu and Iru Agwu — and why the order matters.
GroundPersonal land, not rented. The bare patch of earth with nothing on it and everything under it. Covenants. Why a landlord's quit notice would dismantle your shrine, and what to do while you do not yet own land.
RootTwo sticks. Not seeds. Cuttings from your mother's tree and your father's tree. A stick is an inheritance, not a beginning. The rite described plainly — two delegations, two holes, two sticks, one week.
TrunkThe dibia who farms. The man does the rite and then goes back to farming. Character, not ceremony, keeps the tree alive. A man who cannot hold a job for two years is not ready to hold a tree for forty.
BranchBound to water them. Your children did not choose your tree. Your job is not to force them to serve it. It is to water it well enough that they can choose it.
FruitHow a tree becomes a god. A man plants a stick alone in his compound. His children tend it. His children's children tend it. Four generations later a whole community sacrifices at that tree. A god is started by a person nobody was watching.
ShadeThe part you will never sit in. You plant trees you will not live to see tall. "A good man leaveth an inheritance to his children's children" is not wisdom about money. It is a description of what a human being is for.
For the person who is far from home

Your ancestors already solved migration.
They did not tell you to come back before you could build.

  • You are not the first to leave. A hundred years ago a man left his compound with a bundle and walked to a town he had never seen. There is a rite for carrying Agwu with you. The compound has been three thousand miles from somebody before.
  • Ime Agwu is available to you today. It requires you to travel, once, to your father's land and your mother's land, and stand on it. That is a fare, a leave application, a phone call to an uncle. It is not land ownership.
  • Land disputes are family problems wearing money costumes. A plot in court for nine years is not a money problem. It is two uncles, one estate, zero honest conversations. You may be the person who starts that conversation.
  • A good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children. Not his children. Two generations past the end of his own life. Cathedral thinking is not new; it is in your book.
This is not a ceremony manual. The book does not teach you to perform a rite. Rites belong to lineage and land, and you enter them through your own people, in person, with somebody who knows what they are doing. If you finish this book able to perform anything, the author has failed. What you get is the shape of the thing, why it exists, what it asks of you, and how to start asking the right people the right questions.

Munachimso E. Nwagbara writes from Mgbowo, Enugu State. He is thirty-three years old.

He has done Ime Agwu — both sides, his father's and his mother's. He has not done Iru Agwu. He is getting ready, and this book is part of that preparation. He is not writing from the far side of anything.

Questions

The ones you are asking.

Do I need to be Igbo or African to read this?

The practice described is specific to Igbo life and land. The argument — that generativity rather than healing is the adult task, and that you plant things you will not sit under — does not belong to one people. Read it for whatever in it is yours.

Will this tell me how to perform Iru Agwu?

No. That is the point. It tells you what it is, what conditions it requires (your own land, both sides, one week, two delegations), and why you cannot fake it on rented ground. It tells you where to start. It does not, and cannot, initiate you.

I do not own land. Is this book for me?

Yes. Stage one is available to you now. Most of you reading this do not own land, and the author knows that. The chapter on ground is written explicitly for people who rent in Lagos, London, Houston, Toronto, Johannesburg or Dubai.

Is this a Christian book? A traditional book?

It holds Proverbs 13:22 alongside Igbo rite and Erik Erikson's developmental research. It does not reconcile them into a system. It puts them next to each other and lets each do what it does.

How long is it?

Roughly 18,000 words — about 85 typeset pages. The author wrote it short on purpose.
